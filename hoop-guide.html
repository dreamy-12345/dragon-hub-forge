Why it isn't just π × the size

Hoops are sold by their outside diameter. The tube bends round its own centre line, and that line runs half a tube in from the outside all the way round. So the centre line's diameter is the hoop size minus one tube width:

cut length = π × (hoop outside − tube outside)

π is 3.1416. Work in one unit: all inches or all mm.

Example: a 36" hoop in 3/4" tube. 36 − 0.75 = 35.25, and 35.25 × 3.1416 = 110.7", which is 281.3 cm. Plain π × 36 would give 113.1": 2.4" too long, and the hoop would come out nearly an inch too big.

The connector goes inside the tube and the two ends butt together, so it adds nothing. Cut exactly the length.

Which size for you?

HoopFor
40–44" heavyLearning to hoop on the waist: big and heavy turns slowly, so you have time.
36–40"Waist and body hooping once you can keep it up.
32–36"Tricks, breaks, a mix of on and off body.
28–32"Fast off-body and hand hooping.
20–28"Minis and doubles (one in each hand). Make two the same.

Quick check for a first hoop: stand it on the floor beside you. It should come up to somewhere between your belly button and your chest. Smaller and lighter is faster and harder.

HDPE is sold by its inside size ("3/4 inch" is the bore), so its outside varies by maker and pressure rating: measure the outside with calipers and type it in. Polypro is sold by its outside size.

Fire hoopsFire hoops need wicks bolted through the tube and the heat stays near the wicks. Make them from tubing sold for fire hoops, with Kevlar wicks and metal fixings only, as in the forge's wicking guide. A plain plastic hoop is never for fire.

What to buy

For one hoop
  • Tubing: the cut length plus 5% for squaring the ends.
  • A connector: print the forge one (PETG or nylon), or buy a push-in hoop connector the size of your tube's bore.
  • A pipe cutter (the ratchet kind cuts clean and square), or a fine saw and sandpaper.
  • 2 small self-tapping screws (about 3 × 10 mm) or 2 small rivets, and a drill bit to match.
  • Tape: grip tape for the inside, decorative tape (gaffer, glitter or holographic) for the outside.
  • A pan or kettle of very hot water.

Making it

  1. Uncoil the tube and let it sit in the sun or a warm room for an hour: it relaxes and is easier to work.
  2. Measure along the outside of the coil with a tape measure and mark the cut length. Measure twice.
  3. Cut square with the pipe cutter. With a saw: sand the end flat and scrape off the burr.
  4. Soften one end: hold it in just-boiled water for about a minute. Careful: hot.
  5. Push it onto the connector until it reaches the notch in the middle. Twist as you push.
  6. Curve it round the way the tube was coiled. Soften the other end and push it on until the two ends meet. No gap.
  7. Fix it: drill a small hole 2 to 3 cm from the joint on each side, through the tube into the connector. Drive in a screw or set a rivet. Keep the heads flush so they don't catch your skin.
  8. Round it: if the hoop is a bit oval, warm it in the sun and lay it flat on the floor under a light weight overnight.
  9. Tape: grip tape on the inside where it rolls on you. Wind decorative tape round the outside at a slant. Start and finish over the joint, and pull it firm so it doesn't wrinkle.
  10. Weigh it. Pairs (minis, doubles): weigh both and trim the tape until they match within 5 g.

Collapsible hoops

A hoop that comes apart travels in a car or a suitcase. Cut it into 2, 4 or 6 equal pieces (the cut length divided by the number of pieces). Put a connector in every joint, with a spring push-button through each one so it clicks together instead of being screwed. Sections from the same coil hold their curve best.
