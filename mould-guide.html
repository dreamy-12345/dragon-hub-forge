Silicone and rubber moulds: cast your own ends

How to use the Dragon Hub Forge moulds to cast contact staff balls, rope dart heads and poi heads in silicone or urethane rubber. You can also make a flexible mould and cast as many copies as you like. Short steps, one job each.

Two ways to make one

Printed mouldPrint a hard two-part mould and pour rubber in. What comes out is your end: soft, grippy, heavy. One print, as many casts as you like. Best for silicone ends.
Mould boxPrint the shape (the master) plus a box. Pour silicone round the master to make a flexible mould, then cast copies in resin, rubber, wax or pewter. Best for hard copies and fine detail.

Pick one in the forge with Mould. The forge gives you every part as an STL, and tells you how much rubber to mix.

Why not cast resin in the printed mould?A hard cast in a hard mould gets stuck on the smallest undercut, and dimples or scales are full of them. One side must bend. Rubber in a printed mould works. A hard cast needs a rubber mould: use the mould box.

Which rubber?

The number with an "A" is the Shore hardness: higher is firmer. Platinum silicone is the one that is skin safe once it is fully cured. It is the right choice for anything you touch, spin or roll on your body.

Liquid latex (brushed on in coats) also makes moulds, but it shrinks, smells and is not for ends people with latex allergy will touch. The forge moulds are made for pourable rubber.

What to buy

For every cast
  • The rubber, two-part (A and B). The forge says how many ml; buy at least 20% more.
  • A digital kitchen scale (1 g steps). Mix by weight, not by eye.
  • Clean plastic cups and flat wooden sticks. Nitrile gloves (not latex).
  • Mould release spray, or petroleum jelly thinned with a little white spirit.
  • Clear spray lacquer, or thin epoxy, to seal the print.
  • Sharp scissors or a craft knife to trim the pour stubs.
Printed mould
  • 4 × M5 bolts with washers and wing nuts (the forge gives the length), or two G-clamps or strong rubber bands.
  • Rope dart or poi with a cast-in nut: one M8 nut, one big M8 washer (the forge says the size), one plain M8 bolt (the forge says the length). Then a stainless M8 swivel eye bolt and thread-locker.
Mould box
  • Sulfur-free plasticine (modelling clay), to seal round the master.
  • 4 × M4 bolts (or 4 mm dowels or skewers) to line the frames up, and 2 short pieces of 1.75 mm filament as dowels for the master.
  • Fine sandpaper (400 to 800) and spray primer for the master.

The one thing that ruins silicone

Platinum silicone stays sticky next to these

Platinum silicone will not cure where it touches:

  • sulfur (most cheap modelling clays);
  • latex gloves;
  • tin silicone;
  • uncured resin prints;
  • some tapes, paints and fresh superglue.

The surface stays gooey forever. The fix:

  • Wear nitrile gloves.
  • Use sulfur-free clay.
  • Seal the print and let the sealer dry fully (a day).
  • Resin prints: wash them, then cure them under UV for twice as long as usual.
  • Before a big pour, test a teaspoon of silicone on a scrap of the same print.

Printing the mould

  1. PLA or PETG, never TPU: the mould must stay rigid or the cast comes out lopsided.
  2. Print each half split face up, as the forge lays it out. No supports: the cavity is a bowl.
  3. 5 walls and 30% infill. Thin walls let liquid rubber seep through the layer gaps into the infill: thick walls stop it and keep the halves stiff.
  4. 0.12 mm layers (0.16 at most). Every layer line in the cavity shows on the cast.
  5. Check the split faces are flat: sand off any blobs, or the halves leak.
  6. Seal the cavity with two thin coats of brushing epoxy (made for smoothing prints), or clear lacquer. Let it dry fully: a day. This fills the layer lines and stops rubber soaking in and sticking. Keep the coats thin so the seal ridge still fits its groove.
  7. Test first: a teaspoon of your rubber on a scrap printed and sealed the same way. If it is still sticky after the cure time, the sealer is stopping it curing: change sealer.
  8. Rope darts and poi: the halves carry the moulded eye pins, the nut channel and the vents. Clear any strings so the vents are open.

Way 1: printed mould, cast the end

  1. Release. Spray or brush mould release on both halves, all of the cavity, the funnel, and the core pin. Let it flash off for a few minutes.
  2. Cast-in nut (darts and poi): screw the M8 nut onto the plain bolt, big washer on top of the nut. Lay the bolt in the channel of one half, head on top of the mould. The washer and nut sit in the cavity.
  3. Close it. Put the halves together on the keys: the ridge round the cavity on half A drops into the groove on half B. Bolt (or clamp) evenly in a cross pattern, snug, not crushing.
  4. Core pin (contact balls): push the printed pin down through the top until its flange sits on the mould. This makes the pole socket.
  5. Stand it funnel up on a level tray lined with a bin bag.
  6. Mix the rubber (see mixing). The forge says how much: mix 15% extra for the funnel.
  7. Pour slowly, in one thin unbroken stream, into the funnel. With bottom fill (the default) the rubber runs down the sprue beside the part and comes in at its lowest point, then rises and pushes the air out of the vents ahead of it. Do not stop and start: every pause folds in air. Keep going until rubber comes up every vent and sits in the funnel.
  8. Top up after 5 minutes: the level in the funnel drops as the last air leaves. The funnel is also the reservoir that feeds the part as it settles.
  9. Patterned ones (Dimple, Geo, Dragon Scales, Geo Poi): open the mould first, brush a thin coat of rubber into every dip, wait 10 minutes, then close and pour. This stops air being trapped in the detail.
  10. Wait the full cure time on the label (usually 4 to 24 hours), at room temperature. Cold makes it slower.
  11. Open it. Pull out the core pin, or unscrew the bolt. Take out the bolts and lever the halves apart with a screwdriver in the slots at the bottom corners.
  12. Trim the gate (where the runner met the bottom of the part), the vent threads and the pour stub flush with sharp scissors or a fresh blade. The seal ridge keeps the seam line very thin: trim it with the scissors flat.
  13. Post cure (silicone ends you hold): a few hours somewhere warm (around 50 to 60 °C, an oven on its lowest setting with the door ajar, or a hot car) drives off what is left and makes it fully skin safe and odourless.
  14. Cast-in nut: screw in a stainless M8 swivel eye bolt with a drop of medium thread-locker. Tug it hard. It must not pull out.

Way 2: mould box, make a rubber mould

Make the master

  1. Print the two master halves split face down (they lie flat).
  2. Glue them together with superglue, a short piece of filament in each of the two dowel holes to line them up. Let it dry overnight. Fresh glue stops platinum silicone curing.
  3. Sand smooth (400 then 800 grit), spray primer, sand again. The rubber copies every scratch.
  4. Seal with clear coat and let it dry fully.

First pour

  1. Put one frame flat on the table. Put the parting board on it. Line them up with the bolts or dowels through the corner ears.
  2. Press the master into the board's cut-out until its seam is level with the board's top face. Rope darts with a moulded eye: the little plug that printed with the board goes in the eye hole.
  3. Seal any gap round the master with a thin roll of sulfur-free plasticine, smoothed flat.
  4. Put the second frame on top of the board. Spray release on the board, the frame and the master.
  5. Mix the rubber and pour it into a low corner, letting it flow over the master. Fill until it covers the highest point by 10 mm. The forge gives the total for both halves: this pour is half.
  6. Let it cure fully.

Second pour

  1. Turn the whole thing over. Lift off the bottom frame and the board. Pick out the plasticine. The master stays in the first rubber half, its seam level with the rubber.
  2. Put a frame back on top of the first one.
  3. Coat the rubber with release: brush on a thin layer of petroleum jelly, or use a silicone-on-silicone release. Silicone sticks to silicone like glue. Miss this and you have a solid block.
  4. The registration keys are already there: little bumps the board's pits made. They will make sockets in the second half.
  5. Mix and pour the second half, 10 mm over the master. Let it cure fully.
  6. Take off the frames, peel the halves apart and lift out the master. Keep it: it can make another mould.

Casting copies

  1. Put the rubber halves together on their keys. Put a flat board on each side and wrap rubber bands round them (firm, not squashing). The pour channel and vents point up.
  2. Ball: push the printed core pin into its hole. Dart or poi with a nut: screw the nut and washer onto the bolt and set it in its channel.
  3. Pour your casting material slowly into the channel until the vents fill.

Mixing and bubbles

  1. Read the label: it gives the mix ratio (often 1:1 or 10:1 by weight), working time and cure time.
  2. Weigh part A into a cup, then part B into the same cup.
  3. Stir slowly for 3 minutes, scraping the sides and the bottom. Unmixed streaks never cure.
  4. Pour it into a second clean cup and stir another minute. This gets the sticky bit off the first cup's walls.
  5. Let it sit a minute so bubbles rise. A vacuum chamber (degassing) removes them all: pull vacuum until the rubber rises, collapses and goes quiet (2 to 3 minutes), in a cup four times bigger than the rubber. Not needed for a good cast, but it is the step that makes them flawless.
  6. Urethane: do not vacuum it (it foams and kicks); a pressure pot (about 4 bar) squashes its bubbles too small to see while it cures.
  7. Pour from high up in a thin stream: the stream stretches and pops the bubbles.
  8. Colour: a few drops of silicone pigment, mixed into part A before adding B. Glow powder works too.

Making it heavier

  • Silicone with steel shot: weigh the mixed silicone. Stir in about twice its weight of fine steel shot (0.5 to 1 mm), then pour at once. The shot sinks, so turn the mould slowly end over end for the first 10 minutes, until the silicone thickens. Pick "heavy: silicone 30A with steel shot" in the forge to see the weight.
  • A weight inside: hang a steel nut stack or a ball bearing on the core pin, or on the bolt, so rubber surrounds it.
  • Bigger: a solid 85 mm silicone ball is already about 330 g; 100 mm is about 550 g.
  • Steel, not lead: lead is toxic to handle.

Fitting the finished end

  • Contact balls: the socket is a millimetre under your pole so the rubber grips it. Wet the pole end with a drop of soapy water and push the ball on with a twist. Once dry it holds. For a permanent fit, use a silicone-safe adhesive.
  • Rope darts: tie the rope to the swivel with a figure-eight loop or a bowline. Check the swivel and the nut before every session.
  • Poi: clip the leash to the swivel. Make a pair from the same mix and weigh them: within 5 g of each other.
  • Moulded eye: only in 50A silicone or urethane rubber. Check it for splits every session and retire it at the first crack.

What the forge builds in

Moulds are easy to get almost right and hard to get right. These are the things that usually go wrong, and what each forge mould does about them:

  • Bottom fill. Pouring straight in from the top folds air in and traps it. A sprue runs down beside the part and a runner climbs into its lowest point, so the rubber rises from the bottom and pushes the air ahead of it and out of the vents.
  • Vents at every air trap. Air rising through the rubber stops at any ceiling that is a local high point: the top of a dome, under a ridge, round the core pin. The forge finds them by testing the shape on a fine grid and runs a vent from each one to the top. Level rings and flat ceilings get one vent each. In a mould box, traps away from the seam are marked on the master: cut a fine vent from each with a blade.
  • A pointed core pin with draft. A flat pin end is a ceiling that holds a bubble at the bottom of the socket. The pin is pointed (flat only where coins are taped on), and it tapers very slightly (0.4°) so it pulls out of the rubber.
  • A seal ridge. A low ridge runs round the cavity on half A and seats in a groove in half B. It stops leaks and keeps the seam on the cast to a hairline.
  • Shrink allowance. Rubbers and resins shrink as they cure: platinum silicone about 0.1%, urethane about 0.3%, tin silicone about 0.5%, casting wax about 1%. The cavity is made that much bigger, so the part comes out true to size.
  • Undercut check. A rigid mould only lets go of surfaces that face away from the seam. The forge measures how much of the part faces back towards it. Soft rubber flexes out; with firm rubber it warns you to open slowly or use the mould box.
  • Keys, bolts and pry slots. 3 or 4 keys line the halves up, 4 bolts clamp them evenly, and both stay clear of the seal ridge and the sprue (the block grows to make room). Slots at the bottom corners take a screwdriver to split it.

Safety

Safety
  • Work in fresh air, with nitrile gloves and glasses.
  • Urethane resin and urethane rubber give off fumes while you mix them: wear an organic-vapour respirator, keep the room aired, and keep them off your skin.
  • Molten pewter: outdoors, leather gloves, goggles, closed shoes, a bone-dry mould, and never near water.
  • Keep it all away from children, pets and food: use cups and sticks only for casting.
  • Read the safety sheet that comes with your rubber.

These are starting points. Every rubber is a little different, so the label on yours wins.
