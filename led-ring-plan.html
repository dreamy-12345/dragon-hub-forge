LED snap-on weight ring

A build plan for a lit version of the one-piece TPU hoop from the Dragon Hub Forge (hub.js, "snap-on weight"). The plan uses 5 V addressable LEDs, an ESP32-C3, and one 300 mAh LiPo, and every electronic part can be bought in Thailand. Prices were checked on 21 Oct 2026.

Key choices
  • LEDs: a 5 mm-wide WS2812B-2020 strip at about 100 LEDs/m, which gives about 78 LEDs round the hoop. The strip faces outward through a 1.2 mm window of natural TPU. If you can't find a 5 mm strip, the fallback is a 10 mm, 144/m WS2812B strip that is in stock in Thailand, but it only fits a deeper, heavier ring.
  • Where the strip goes: in the mass block under the rim, not in the outer wall. The current hoop is already Ø253.8 mm, and a channel in the outer wall would push it past a 256 mm bed.
  • How it goes in: drop it in loose during a print pause. The strip and two power wires go into open slots, and the printer then roofs them over. If you swap to natural TPU before the block, the whole lit zone glows.
  • Electronics: they sit in two flat "pod tabs" 180° apart, tucked in under the hub. Pod A holds the battery, charger and master switch. Pod B holds the ESP32-C3, the mode button and trim weight. Match the two pods' masses to within 1 g.
  • Power: the battery feeds the strip directly at 3.0–4.2 V, with no boost converter and no level shifter. Current is capped at 450 mA in firmware. Expect about 1–2.5 h per charge, or twice that with a second cell in pod B.
  • Water: treat it as splash-proof, not dip-proof. Bubble mix creeps further than water does. Seal the strip inside the print, conformal-coat every board, use a greased press-fit lid, and never charge it wet.

1. Spin and impact numbers

2. Which LEDs

Which way the light should point

The hub spins about the pole. When the staff lies across the body, the audience sees each hub edge-on, so they mostly see the outer face of the rim. That makes radially outward the main direction for the light. Natural TPU also scatters some light down through the block, which lights the face when the staff points toward the crowd.

A normal top-emitting strip wrapped round the inside of a cylinder points outward by itself. It bends the easy way, flat-side, and needs no tricks.

Count and current

  • The slot runs round a circle of about 785 mm, less roughly 30 mm of dark gap at pod B. That leaves about 755 mm of strip, which is 75–78 LEDs at 100/m. Count your own strip after you fit it and set NUM_LEDS to match.
  • A 2020-size LED draws roughly 12–15 mA at full white on a LiPo, about a third of a 5050. So 78 of them at full white would be about 1.1 A. Firmware caps the strip at 450 mA.
  • Idle current is the hidden cost. Each WS2812-type chip draws about 0.5–1 mA even when dark (measure yours, it varies by maker). For 78 LEDs that is 40–80 mA all the time the strip has power. That's why the v1 plan has a hard master switch, and why fewer LEDs gives longer runtime.

4. Electrical design

A single cell runs everything directly. WS2812-type LEDs work from about 3.5–5.3 V, and at a 4.2 V supply their logic threshold is 0.7 × 4.2 = 2.94 V, so the ESP32's 3.3 V data signal is read correctly with no level shifter. The ESP32-C3 Super Mini takes the battery on its 5V pin, where its onboard ME6211 LDO holds 3.3 V down to about 3.4 V.

Limits and settings

  • Charge current: stock TP4056 boards charge at 1 A (R_PROG = 1.2 kΩ), which is 3.3C into a 300 mAh cell and too much. Swap R_PROG for 5.1 kΩ, which gives I ≈ 1200/R ≈ 235 mA, or 0.8C. On these boards R_PROG is the small 0603 resistor next to the chip (usually R3 or "122"); check yours.
  • Current cap: set FastLED.setMaxPowerInVoltsAndMilliamps(4, 450). Small pouch cells are usually rated 1–2C continuous, and short strobe peaks are fine.
  • Battery cutoff: the DW01 doesn't cut out until about 2.4 V, which is hard on the cell. Firmware dims to a red pulse at 3.45 V and goes dark at 3.30 V. After that, slide the master switch off, because the strip's idle current keeps draining the cell.
  • Colour at low voltage: blue and green dim a little below about 3.5 V. That's normal on a single cell.
  • Divider: 100k/100k puts 2.1 V on the ADC at a full 4.2 V, which is inside the C3's roughly 2.5 V range at 11 dB attenuation. It draws 21 µA.
  • Pins: avoid GPIO2, 8 and 9, which are strapping pins (8 is also the onboard LED). GPIO0–5 can wake the chip from deep sleep, which matters for v2.

5. Fitting it into the TPU ring

The hub.js defaults are: rim R = 125 mm and 9 × 9 mm; hoop bore Rin = 124.5 (a fit of −1 mm on the diameter); outer wall 2.4 mm, so Rout = 126.9 (Ø253.8 mm); lip reaching 3 mm in and 1.6 mm thick; and a block that reaches 8.5 mm in under the rim, at depth 2, 6 or 12 mm, giving roughly 49, 90 or 152 g.

Why the strip can't go in the outer wall: snapInfo.fitsBed is already at its limit, with Ø253.8 against 254 allowed on a 256 mm bed. A slot plus a window in the wall would add at least 3 mm to the radius, making Ø ≈ 260. So the strip goes into the mass block below the rim, inside the existing outline. That also means the light (2 mm) ring can't be an LED ring. LED rings start at a block depth of about 10 mm.

Print-pause method (recommended for v1)

  1. Print the hoop as now, block-down. Add slicer pauses at zprint = D − 1.5 (the top of the slot and groove) using M600 or the "pause at layer" option. The slicer should show both cavities open at the top on that layer.
  2. Filament swap (optional but worth it): print the block in natural or clear TPU, then switch to your colour at the pause. The whole lit zone glows and the lip keeps your colour.
  3. At the pause, lay the sleeved strip into the slot. It curls into a hoop on its own, and its springiness presses it against the front wall. Press it to the slot floor with a wooden stick. Put the two groove wires in, and leave the ends coiled in the pod cavities.
  4. Check nothing stands above the layer: run a fingertip round it. Resume. Print the first two roof layers slowly (about 15 mm/s) with the fan at 100%.
  5. Pods: their cavities are open on the bed side (§ pod tabs below), so the only ceiling the printer has to bridge is the pod roof, about 24 mm. At the same pause, lay a pre-cut 0.5 mm PET or acetate plate (a clear blister-pack offcut works) over each pod cavity so the roof prints onto it. If you'd rather not, enable supports for the pod cavities only.

Two-part alternative

Print a coloured base hoop with the slot open to the bottom face, so there is no pause and no bridge. Then close it with a separately printed clear TPU cover annulus about 1.2 mm thick. Bond the cover with flexible PU glue (E6000 or shoe glue) or weld it with a soldering iron and TPU filament. This version is easy to service and lets you use a side-view strip shining down through the clear face.

Downside: the hoop is stretched every time you fit it, and a glue line that is flexed and stretched over and over in bubble mix is the first thing to leak or peel. Use it for v2 only if v1 proves the electronics work.

The strip can't stretch, so let it slide

  • Hoop strain when worn: ΔR = 0.5 on R ≈ 125, so the slot's circumference grows 0.4%, about 3.1 mm round the loop. Flex-PCB copper fatigues if it is repeatedly strained around 0.2–0.3%, so the strip must never be bonded to the TPU.
  • Fix: sleeve the strip in clear heat-shrink, shrunk just enough to grip the strip but not the slot. Make the slot 0.4 mm oversize in height and about 0.8 mm in depth, and use no glue. The strip doesn't form a closed loop: both ends finish in the pods with a 10–15 mm slack S-bend of wire, so the hoop can grow while the strip slides.
  • Fitting the hoop on: going over the lip ovalises and twists the hoop. Both bend the strip its easy way (flat-side), which it tolerates. What it doesn't tolerate is a potato-chip warp, where the hoop bends out of plane and the 5 mm strip bends edgewise; a 300 mm warp radius strains the strip's edges by about 0.8%. So fit it the bike-tyre way the designer already describes: hoop flat on the table, hub in at an angle, then work round it. Never fold the hoop.
  • Consider loosening the fit for LED rings to −0.8 mm. If it then creeps round the rim, add 2–3 small tabs that hook behind spokes, which is better than more stretch.

Pod tabs: where the electronics go

Each pod is a local tab that extends the block inward, under the hub's underside (z < 0, so it can't collide with the rim or web). It is as deep as the block, so it adds no extra depth, and it is about 66 mm long round the arc.

  • Charging: mount the TP4056 so its USB-C faces the inner wall (toward the pole). That's the side most sheltered from floor hits and splash. Give it a tethered TPU plug. The ESP32's own USB-C, for flashing, sits under pod B's lid. After the first upload, ArduinoOTA means you rarely need it.
  • Strain relief: fill each strip-to-wire joint with adhesive-lined heat-shrink. Add a printed U-clip in the pod wall that the wire bundle snaps into, so any tug lands on the clip and not on the solder pads. Anchor every wire within 10 mm of a solder joint.   • Check the hub design: the tabs sit right under the hub's underside. Rotate the ring so the tabs land between spokes, and clear any relief or capsule pods on that face.

6. Balance, impact, water

Balance

  • The strip is spread evenly round the ring, so it adds no imbalance. Only the pods matter. Put them 180° apart and match them within 1 g. One gram of mismatch at r = 0.11 m pulls 0.43 N at 10 rev/s, and moves the centre of mass of a 400 g hub about 0.3 mm. Spinners notice a few grams.
  • Static balance test: fit the hub on a short length of 22 mm pole and rest it on two level, parallel straight edges, such as two rulers on books. Let it roll. The heavy pod settles at the bottom. Add trim (nut fragments or stick-on wheel weight) on the light side until it stays wherever you leave it.
  • For v2 with two cells, the pods balance themselves: A has a cell and the charger, B has a cell and the MCU, and each comes to about 9 g. The cells are wired in parallel through the groove wires. Only parallel two cells at the same voltage, and charge them through the single protected TP4056.

Impact

  • The outer-bottom corner of the block hits first. Chamfer it 1.5 mm, and keep the strip 2.5 mm or more above the floor and behind a 1.2–1.6 mm front window. TPU spreads a hit over several LEDs instead of one.
  • 2020 LEDs are tiny and sit flush on the flex PCB, so they survive well. What breaks are solder joints with a wire levered off them (strain relief above) and the master switch bouncing. A momentary contact break browns out the ESP. Firmware stores the current mode in flash, so a reset comes back in the same mode, and C1 bridges short bounces.
  • Don't pot the LiPo solid in epoxy. Cells swell a little, and a rigid pot then cracks or presses on the cell. Use foam and the pod wall.

Water and bubble mix

  • Bubble mix is water, dish soap, and glycerin or guar. The soap's low surface tension lets it wick into gaps that plain water wouldn't enter. The glycerin is hygroscopic and leaves a conductive, sticky residue. At 4.2 V that is enough to corrode the strip's pads and the ESP over weeks.
  • Layer 1, the print: print the block with at least 3 perimeters, 100% infill (it's a weight anyway) and about 1.05 flow. Printed TPU is close to watertight.
  • Layer 2, the strip: sealed inside the print, plus heat-shrink. The only way in is through the pods.
  • Layer 3, the boards: conformal-coat the ESP32, the TP4056 (mask its USB-C and SW1) and every strip solder joint. Seal the joints with adhesive-lined heat-shrink and neutral-cure silicone.
  • Layer 4, the lids: a greased press-fit seal and a membrane button, with no holes through the TPU.
  • Rules: the LED ring is splash-proof, not for dipping. Keep using the separate bolt-on bubble ring for dipping, or keep the LED hub out of the bucket. After a bubble session, rinse with fresh water, shake it out, and dry it with the lids open before charging. Never charge a wet pod.

7. Firmware and modes

What looks good when it spins

Every LED on a ring that spins about its own centre travels the same circle. A persistence-of-vision image can't build up across the radius the way it does on a spinning bar. At speed you see the ring's average colour, unless you freeze it in time or move the pattern against the spin. That leads to these modes:

ModeWhat you see spinningNotes
HaloA clean single-colour circle that slowly crossfadesFits the soft, airy look. Lowest power.
Palette driftColours mix into a shifting tint, and resolve into bands as it slowsUse your dome-palettes.json gradients.
Strobe-freezeA crisp crown of 6 coloured segments, apparently frozen and jittering, over a dark hubThe showpiece, and it needs no sensor. See the frame-time limit below.
CometA head with a tail running against the spin. Near matching speeds it crawls slowly, like a wagon-wheel effect.Speed is adjustable; long-press cycles it.

Half and halfTwo-tone when slow, blending into a third colour when fastSimple, and readable edge-on.
  • Frame time sets how short a flash can be. WS2812 data takes about 30 µs per LED, so 78 LEDs take about 2.4 ms. At 6 rev/s the rim moves 11 mm in that time, so strobe segments smear by about one LED pitch. That's acceptable. If you want sharper strobes, use fewer LEDs or a clocked APA102/SK9822 strip (I couldn't verify one in Thailand).
  • Turn off temporal dithering with FastLED.setDither(0). On a moving prop, dithering and low PWM duty (WS2812 PWM runs at about 1–2 kHz) show up as dashed trails. You can also lean into that and use it as a look on purpose.
  • v2, true stationary images: to keep the pattern still in space while the hub spins, you need the spin angle. Typical gyros (MPU6050, BMI270, ICM-42688) saturate at 2000 °/s, which is 5.5 rev/s, below contact-roll speeds. A ±32 g accelerometer (LSM6DSO32) on the ring reads centripetal acceleration up to about 8 rev/s. Treat this as an experiment, not v1.
  • v2 sync: ESP-NOW between the two hubs on a staff, or with a phone, at a cost of about 60–80 mA while it listens. LED capsules use an nRF24-style 250 kbit radio that the ESP32 can't speak; see the lighttoys hoop notes.
  • The preview in leds.js already emulates the LED capsule modes as colour over time. Any mode that looks good there as a "lantern" also works as Halo or Palette drift here.

Starter sketch (Arduino IDE, board "ESP32C3 Dev Module", USB CDC On Boot: Enabled, FastLED 3.7 or later)

#include <FastLED.h>
#include <Preferences.h>

#define NUM_LEDS  78      // count yours after fitting
#define DATA_PIN  7
#define BTN_PIN   3       // tact switch to GND
#define VBAT_PIN  1       // middle of 100k/100k divider
#define MAX_MA    450     // whole-ring current cap for a 300 mAh cell
#define MODES     6

CRGB leds[NUM_LEDS];
Preferences prefs;
uint8_t mode = 0, bright = 140, hue = 0, cometSpeed = 3;
bool lowBatt = false, flat = false;

float vbat() { return analogReadMilliVolts(VBAT_PIN) * 2 / 1000.0f; }

void setup() {
  pinMode(BTN_PIN, INPUT_PULLUP);
  analogSetPinAttenuation(VBAT_PIN, ADC_11db);
  prefs.begin("ring", false);
  mode = prefs.getUChar("mode", 0) % MODES;       // survives a brown-out reset
  FastLED.addLeds<WS2812B, DATA_PIN, GRB>(leds, NUM_LEDS);
  FastLED.setMaxPowerInVoltsAndMilliamps(4, MAX_MA);
  FastLED.setDither(0);
  FastLED.setBrightness(bright);
}

void button() {                                    // short = next mode, long = alt setting
  static uint32_t down = 0; static bool was = false;
  bool now = digitalRead(BTN_PIN) == LOW;
  if (now && !was) down = millis();
  if (!now && was) {
    uint32_t held = millis() - down;
    if (held > 30 && held < 700) { mode = (mode + 1) % MODES; prefs.putUChar("mode", mode); }
    else if (held >= 700) {
      if (mode == 3) cometSpeed = cometSpeed % 8 + 1;
      else { bright = bright == 140 ? 50 : 140; FastLED.setBrightness(bright); }
    }
  }
  was = now;
}

void battery() {
  float v = vbat();
  lowBatt = v < 3.45f;
  flat = v < 3.30f;
  if (lowBatt) FastLED.setBrightness(min<uint8_t>(bright, 40));
}

void loop() {
  button();
  EVERY_N_SECONDS(5) battery();
  EVERY_N_MILLISECONDS(60) hue++;
  if (flat) {                                      // dark: now slide the master switch off
    fill_solid(leds, NUM_LEDS, CRGB::Black); FastLED.show(); delay(200); return;
  }
  switch (mode) {
    case 0:  // halo
      fill_solid(leds, NUM_LEDS, CHSV(hue, 200, beatsin8(6, 120, 255)));
      break;
    case 1:  // palette drift
      fill_rainbow(leds, NUM_LEDS, hue * 2, 255 / NUM_LEDS * 2);
      break;
    case 2: { // strobe-freeze: one frame of 6 segments every ~35 ms, dark between
      static uint32_t t0 = 0;
      fill_solid(leds, NUM_LEDS, CRGB::Black);
      if (millis() - t0 > 35) {
        t0 = millis();
        for (int i = 0; i < NUM_LEDS; i++) leds[i] = CHSV(hue + (i * 6 / NUM_LEDS) * 43, 255, 255);
        FastLED.show();                           // lit for about one frame time
        fill_solid(leds, NUM_LEDS, CRGB::Black);
      }
      break; }
    case 3: { // comet against the spin
      static uint16_t pos = 0;
      fadeToBlackBy(leds, NUM_LEDS, 40);
      pos = (pos + NUM_LEDS - 1) % NUM_LEDS;
      for (uint8_t k = 1; k < cometSpeed; k++) pos = (pos + NUM_LEDS - 1) % NUM_LEDS;
      leds[pos] = CHSV(hue, 180, 255);
      break; }
    case 4:  // fireflies
      fadeToBlackBy(leds, NUM_LEDS, 12);
      if (random8() < 40) leds[random16(NUM_LEDS)] = CHSV(hue + random8(40), 160, 255);
      break;
    case 5:  // half and half
      for (int i = 0; i < NUM_LEDS; i++) leds[i] = CHSV(i < NUM_LEDS / 2 ? hue : hue + 128, 230, 220);
      break;
  }
  if (lowBatt && (millis() / 1000) % 10 == 0) leds[0] = CRGB::Red;   // red blip every 10 s
  FastLED.show();
  delay(4);
}

I haven't compiled this sketch. It is written against FastLED 3.7's API for ESP32-C3 (RMT), so expect to fix small details on the first build. v2 additions: a long hold of more than 3 s pulls GPIO4 low to cut the strip's power through the AO3401, then the ESP calls esp_deep_sleep_enable_gpio_wakeup(1ULL<<3, ESP_GPIO_WAKEUP_GPIO_LOW) and sleeps. That draws about 50 µA (LDO plus chip, with the red LED removed), so the master switch is then only needed for storage and flights.

9. Build checklist

  1. Order: the controller, charger and battery from the verified shops, two strips (one spare), and the consumables. Do steps 2–4 while the strip ships.
  2. Bench electronics: swap R_PROG to 5.1 kΩ and check a charge at about 240 mA with a USB meter. Remove the Super Mini's red power LED. Wire everything on the bench with the strip laid out flat. Flash the sketch.
  3. Measure (T1): current with all LEDs dark, at Halo, and at full white. Put the real idle figure into the runtime table. Count the LEDs that fit in 755 mm.
  4. Designer: add the LED variant to hub.js (§12). Export it. Slice with 3+ perimeters, 100% infill, and a pause at the top of the slot. If you're swapping filament, put the change at the same pause.
  5. Dry run (T2): print a 60 mm arc segment of the ring's cross-section first. Test the pause, the strip fit, the roof bridge, and how a pod lid snaps.
  6. Prepare the strip: cut it to length at the copper pads. Solder 26 AWG to +V and GND at both ends and 30 AWG to DIN at the start. Use adhesive heat-shrink on each joint, slide the strip into clear heat-shrink and shrink it lightly. Conformal-coat the joints.
  7. Print and pause: lay in the strip and groove wires, put the acetate plates over the pods, and resume. Don't touch the hot bed area with the wire ends; tape them up out of the way.
  8. Fit out the pods: foam, cell and TP4056 in A; ESP32, C1, button and trim in B. Clip the wires into the strain-relief U-clips. Conformal-coat the boards (mask the USB-C ports and the switch). Fill the remaining voids with small foam pieces so nothing can rattle.
  9. Light test before closing the lids. Then grease and close the lids.
  10. Balance: do the static roll test and trim pod B.
  11. Fit it on the hub the bike-tyre way, flat on the table without folding it. Light test again.
  12. Run the test plan below before taking it to a jam.
